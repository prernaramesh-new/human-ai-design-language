Design notes & research.

The principles, demonstration briefs, alternatives, and research behind the story. The proposed designs remain separate from the evidence that informed them.

01 · What is the AI doing?

Generation

PurposeMake it clear when a reply is being prepared and when it arrives.

Human principlePeople need to know that their request has been heard.

Demonstration briefShow a short acknowledgement, the reply being prepared, then the reply arriving in readable beats.

RepresentationsBadge · Line · Music · Robot. These remain alternatives; no family is selected by the story.

  • Music: Keep the background sound low beneath the AI’s voice while it replies. Speech should remain clear.
  • Robot: Show the AI speaking while its background sound drops below the voice.

What to preserveUse a short status label alongside the signal. Avoid making people read a stream of individual words.

Working

PurposeShow the kind of work taking place without promising a finish time the system cannot know.

Human principlePeople judge a wait differently when they understand what is happening.

Demonstration briefReserve space for Processing, Retrieving, and Calculating, plus the transition for correcting earlier work.

RepresentationsBadge · Line · Music · Robot. These remain alternatives; no family is selected by the story.

  • Music: Use the waiting sound at a faster pace to signal work. Lower it whenever someone speaks.
  • Robot: Make the sound busier while work continues, and lower it whenever someone speaks.

What to preserveOnly show measurable progress when the system has a real measure. A correction returns to work; an error remains stopped.

Waiting

PurposeShow that the AI is ready for you, with no answer still in progress.

Human principlePeople need to know when the next move is theirs.

Demonstration briefShow a quiet, available state that is clearly different from both working and being stuck.

RepresentationsBadge · Line · Music · Robot. These remain alternatives; no family is selected by the story.

  • Music: Hold a quiet, continuous sound during the gap between turns. It should work for a short or long wait.
  • Robot: Keep the sound slow and unhurried, clearly different from active work.

What to preserveKeep the signal near the input. Its pace should feel calmer than active work.

Listening

PurposeAcknowledge that the AI is receiving what you are saying.

Human principlePeople need a sign that their contribution is being received.

Demonstration briefShow attention while the person speaks. In voice, keep the sound below the conversation.

RepresentationsBadge · Line · Music · Robot. These remain alternatives; no family is selected by the story.

  • Music: Lower the background sound while the person speaks. Listening and speaking use the same quiet treatment.
  • Robot: Lower the background sound while the person speaks, leaving their voice clear.

What to preserveWaiting and listening must remain understandable with motion turned off. Use words as well as the signal.

Handover

PurposeMake the change from AI to human clear, so people know who is answering.

Human principlePeople need to know who is responsible for the exchange.

Demonstration briefShow the AI passing the conversation to a person, with a brief overlap before the AI leaves.

RepresentationsBadge · Line · Robot. These remain alternatives; no family is selected by the story.

  • Robot: Use a short sound cue to mark the moment a person takes over from the AI.

What to preserveThe AI and person are separate participants. The transition should never suggest that one becomes the other.

Yielding

PurposeWhen you interrupt, the AI stops its reply and acknowledges your turn.

Human principlePeople need to be able to redirect a conversation.

Demonstration briefShow an active reply yielding when a message is sent or a voice interruption is detected.

RepresentationsBadge · Line · Robot. These remain alternatives; no family is selected by the story.

  • Robot: Use a brief sound cue to acknowledge a voice interruption as the AI yields the turn.

What to preserveTyping a draft does not interrupt the AI. Keep the interrupted reply visible so the conversation retains its context.

Error handling

PurposeShow what stopped, explain what went wrong, and give the person a way forward.

Human principlePeople need to understand a failure before they can recover from it.

Demonstration briefKeep the interrupted work recognisable. Pair the stopped signal with a short explanation and a recovery action.

RepresentationsBadge · Line. These remain alternatives; no family is selected by the story.

What to preserveApply this treatment to work in progress. Missing information is not automatically an error.

Orienting

PurposeShow what has been covered, where you are now, and what may come next.

Human principlePeople find their way more easily when they can see their place in the whole.

Demonstration briefShow Covered, Now, and Next together. Let the next steps change as the conversation develops.

RepresentationsBadge · Line. These remain alternatives; no family is selected by the story.

What to preserveUse “Progress” in the interface. Future steps are a guide, not a promise of a fixed route.

Notes

PurposeKeep the information gathered in one place, including facts nobody planned to ask for.

Human principlePeople can follow a conversation more easily when they do not have to remember every detail.

Demonstration briefStart with the information being sought. Fill it in as it arrives, and add volunteered facts with their values.

RepresentationsBadge · Line. These remain alternatives; no family is selected by the story.

What to preserveCount only the planned questions. Keep additional facts visible without treating them as unfinished checklist items.

02 · What can it access or change?

Check the sources

PurposeConnect an answer to the records, inputs, and calculations behind it.

Human principlePeople need to see the basis of a claim before deciding how much to trust it.

Demonstration briefShow how someone can follow a claim to its source, inspect a calculation, or change the inputs.

OptionsPull a thread · Lift the number · Put the sources on trial

  • Each figure in the answer draws a thread to the system that produced it, and the one source it never reached stands alone.
  • Lift a figure out of the sentence and it separates into what it means, how it was calculated, and which system it came from.
  • Pull a source out of the evidence and the answer recalculates: some claims still hold, others no longer stand.

ExampleA campaign manager asks why customers abandoned checkout and what a WhatsApp offer would cost, and which figures came from where.

What to preserveSeparate evidence the AI used from information it could access. An explanation alone is not proof.

Further readingNielsen · Usability heuristics ↗
NIST · Privacy Framework ↗

See what AI can access

PurposeMake the boundary clear between available information, information already read, and restricted information.

Human principlePeople need to understand who can reach their information and where that access ends.

Demonstration briefShow each source as the AI reaches for it, let the person refuse one while it is being read, and show what stays out of reach.

OptionsBuilding blocks · The vault

  • The answer is built from one block per source, laid down while you watch. A block can be pulled out while it is still arriving, and the answer stands on the blocks that are left.
  • What you close is sealed into a vault that already holds what the AI can never do, like sending mail or searching the web. Anything in it can be taken back out.

ExampleAn operations manager asks what poor agent behaviour is costing; the AI reaches for payroll data nobody asked for and is refused.

What to preserveA connected source is not necessarily loaded into the current task. Keep restricted content out of the demonstration.

Set permission limits

PurposeLet people understand the task, the information involved, and how long permission lasts.

Human principlePeople make informed choices when the scope and consequences are clear.

Demonstration briefShow exactly what a permission lets through, before anything reaches the AI, whether the source is a shared file or a connected system.

OptionsThe filter · The live permission sentence

  • The source becomes a list of named fields, and only what passes the filter reaches the AI. Possible personal data is marked.
  • The permission is a sentence that can be read aloud, each phrase bound to the data beneath it. Edit either side and the other follows.

ExampleA team lead is asked for September’s staffing numbers, from a workbook that also holds salaries and the team lead’s own notes on people.

What to preserveDistinguish reading, drafting, and sending. Permission for one does not grant the others.

Preview a change

PurposeShow what a change will affect before it is made.

Human principlePeople can make better choices when they can see likely consequences first.

Demonstration briefShow every connected system a change will reach, as a heads-up rather than a block, with an optional test call. Nothing goes live until the person confirms.

OptionsThe ripple · The dominoes

  • The change sits at the centre and travels outward as a wave. What it reaches answers, and distance from the centre is distance from the change.
  • Each domino is something the change pushes over, with the fix that would stop it underneath. The change travels; the damage stops.

ExampleA contact-centre manager adds a store-credit step to the refund voice bot; its report, its dashboard and the revenue figure all move with it.

What to preservePreviewing makes no changes. If the underlying information changes, the preview must be checked again.

Review the result

PurposeShow everything an action changed, including anything it took out.

Human principlePeople need clear feedback on what an action actually did.

Demonstration briefShow every change the AI made to finished work, so the one that matters is found by looking rather than by rereading every sentence.

OptionsThe blacklight · The printing press

  • A band of light sweeps the finished script and everything the AI touched fluoresces. The line it removed lights as an outline with nothing inside.
  • Each confirmed edit strikes one slug of type into the original script. One comes down blank, leaving the removed line sitting in an outlined space.

ExampleA manager asks for the refund bot to sound warmer with the policy unchanged; the revision also drops the line telling callers they can still have a refund.

What to preserveShow a removal as plainly as an addition. Untouched material stays quiet, so what changed stands out.

Further readingShneiderman · Eight golden rules ↗

Stop or undo

PurposeLet people stop further work and reverse completed changes where possible.

Human principlePeople act more confidently when they know how to recover.

Demonstration briefDistinguish stopping future actions, undoing reversible changes, and correcting an action that cannot be undone.

OptionsThe breakaway coupling · The wet ink · The zipper

  • Every action passes through one coupling. Stopping separates it, and the scheduler, not the disconnected AI, runs the undo.
  • A booking is ink on the surface and lifts away however old it is. A delivered message is pressed into the paper and stays.
  • The zip closes over work as it completes. Undo runs it backwards, except at a clasp marked Delivered.

ExampleA team lead stops a run of callback bookings made for the wrong day: one confirmation delivered, one queued, one booking in progress.

What to preserveMake irreversible actions clear before commitment. A message that has been sent cannot be unsent by changing a status. The AI may draft the correction, but only the person can send it.

03 · How do I return to my work?

Find your work

PurposeHelp people return through the plan, reply, or report they recognise.

Human principleRecognising a familiar detail is easier than remembering an exact name.

Demonstration briefShow one search over a person’s own past chats that works out what kind of memory the query is, and answers in a form to fit it: a timeline for a time, a contact sheet for something made, excerpts for a phrase, a sourced answer for a question, people for who, a checklist for a routine.

ExampleA supervisor remembers a chat about pickup rates from about two months ago. It was eleven weeks.

What to preserveKeep the work connected to its conversation. Distinguish archived work from work that is deleted or unavailable.

Further readingNielsen · Usability heuristics ↗

Pick up where you left off

PurposeBring the goal, agreed decisions, and unfinished step back into view.

Human principlePeople resume more easily when they do not have to reconstruct what happened.

Demonstration briefShow the work holding the person’s place: what is still open gathers where they stopped, and opens on what changed when they return.

OptionsFold in a plan · Paper fold in a chat · Lifting veil in a chat

  • A callback plan folds at the unfinished decision, collects a staffing update while the person is away, and settles flat once they decide.
  • In an open chat, everything still open goes under one paper fold where they stopped, and it opens by itself when they return.
  • A vellum veil settles over the open items, with one corner turned up on what changed, and the person lifts it.

ExampleThe callback audience is agreed; the time window is still open, and a new staffing note has arrived. In the open chat: a question nobody answered, two options still being weighed, and something waiting on a colleague.

What to preserveKeep confirmed choices separate from AI guesses. Show what changed before the person continues.

Find a decision or file

PurposeGive important moments a clear place in a long conversation.

Human principleStable landmarks help people find something again without retracing every step.

Demonstration briefShow a decision or file on a conversation map, then jump to the original message and surrounding context, and return with Back to latest.

ExampleA supervisor’s manager asks why overtime was capped at 6 hours. The decision is days back in a week-long chat.

What to preserveA summary should lead back to its source. Make edited or missing sources visible.

Further readingNielsen · Usability heuristics ↗

Catch up on changes

PurposeShow which sources behind an earlier answer have changed since, and whether the answer still holds.

Human principlePeople keep trusting a conclusion until something tells them its basis has changed, and rarely notice a change they weren’t looking for. They need to know when the situation they decided on has changed, and what state the system is in now.

Demonstration briefReopen a conversation whose sources have changed. Show which file changed, what changed in it, which part of the answer used it, and whether that part still holds.

OptionsThe tray · The paper memo

  • A tray slides out from under the conversation with one slip for each changed file, and the person pulls it open.
  • A single note folded into a paper plane flies in, lands pointing at the answer, and opens out.

ExampleA supervisor asked whether the team can hold 80/20 in November. Since then the forecast has been revised up and the handle-time report re-issued.

What to preserveShow a change that still holds alongside one that breaks the answer, so a change doesn’t read as automatically bad. Only the change that breaks something asks for a decision.

Hand work to someone

PurposeCarry the work into a new conversation, another device, or a colleague’s hands, in a form that fits what they need from it.

Human principleA handoff works when both people understand the situation.

Demonstration briefTurn a long conversation into the parts of the work: a summary, decisions, tasks and their status, open questions, an index of what was made, and a reusable prompt. The person leaves out the parts they won’t share, then sends it.

OptionsSediment in a glass · Origami

  • The conversation pours into a glass and settles into one layer per part, each named inside it. Closing a layer lets the rest settle into its place.
  • The screen itself folds into a shape with a part on each fold. Clicking a fold takes it out and the rest re-form, and the receiver unfolds it into a new conversation.

ExampleAn MIS analyst hands September’s MIS report to a colleague the day before going on leave.

What to preserveFiles are not part of the handover: the outputs index lists names and states. The person leaves out the parts they won’t share.

04 · People & motivation

The project gathered 203 quotes across seven source groups and developed 15 personas. These are interpretations of public discourse, not fixed labels or a representative user study. Quotes were spot-checked rather than individually reverified; some Reddit voices came through journalism. Some needs involve work, policy, or economics beyond interface design. Design work has not started.

It happened to them

The Displaced

Has lost work as AI use has grown.

A paying market, fair compensation, and honesty about what changed.

The Squeezed

Still has the job, with more expected of them.

Time saved to benefit them, rather than becoming a higher workload.

The Leapfrogger

Can now do things that used to be out of reach.

Useful tools that remain accessible and affordable.

They work with it

The Maximalist

Hands whole tasks to AI and finds it useful.

More capable tools and fewer obstacles.

The Verifier

Uses AI often and checks its work closely.

Reliable results, clear sources, and time to review.

The Rebuilder

Has changed their practice around AI.

Support, training, and enough stability to adapt.

The Guilty User

Uses AI while feeling uneasy about relying on it.

Clear guidance on acceptable use and room to retain their skills.

They argue about it

The Inevitabilist

Expects AI to keep expanding.

Support and resources for that expansion.

The Balance-Sheet Skeptic

Questions whether the investment adds up.

Clear costs and evidence of returns.

The Extinction Watcher

Is concerned about severe risks from advanced AI.

Limits on the development of the most powerful systems.

The Harms-Now Advocate

Focuses on damage already being done.

Accountability, consent, and enforcement.

The Craft Guardian

Values the human effort behind creative work.

Clear labels, support for creators, and the choice to refuse AI.

They live alongside it

The Attached

Feels connected to a particular AI experience.

Continuity and a say in changes that affect that experience.

The Irritated Bystander

Dislikes AI being added to products they already use.

An easy way to turn it off.

The Unbothered

Does not see a need for AI in daily life.

The freedom to stay uninvolved.

05 · Assumptions and open questions

What is established, what is a design hypothesis, and where these patterns are known to fall short. Nothing here has been through user testing. These are the places we would most like to be argued with.

[a] Across the design language

Accessibility status: The visual treatments have not yet been evaluated for accessibility. They must remain understandable when motion is reduced or removed, and equivalent status information must be available without relying on animation alone. Two specific shortcomings of the reduced-motion still frames are recorded at [k] and [l]. One exception has been measured: the warning symbol and its background meet the minimum contrast for non-text elements on every background used here, while the frozen mark itself falls below it between blinks. Since the mark keeps its own colour, the symbol is what carries the signal rather than anything about the mark.

[b] Working

Evidence status: The requirement not to imply precision the system does not have is established. The division into three modes, and each of the three visual treatments, are design hypotheses that require user testing — in particular, whether people understand the distinction or see only “busy.”

[c] Waiting

Evidence status: The importance of visible system status and turn-taking is established. The need for a distinct Waiting state, and this visual treatment, are design hypotheses requiring user testing — especially whether people understand it as “your turn” rather than as inactivity, delay or disconnection.

[d] Listening

Important scope: Clark and Brennan support feedback that fits the communication medium, but do not prescribe a microphone indicator. In voice interaction, Listening should mean that audio is actively being captured by or supplied to the system; it should not claim transmission when processing happens on the device. In text interaction, it must not imply access to anything the person has not submitted. Whether one state and visual treatment work across both modes requires user testing.

[e] Orienting

Evidence status: The importance of helping people understand the current state and likely future behaviour of a dynamic automated system is established, primarily in research on safety-critical systems. Applying that principle to orientation within a conversation is an extension. The five-part structure, the generated topic labels and its usefulness when returning to or joining a conversation are design hypotheses requiring user testing.

[f] Yielding

Evidence status: The need for user control and efficient dismissal is established in UX and human–AI design guidance. Treating a submitted message as an interruption, and the visual treatment used here, are design hypotheses requiring testing — particularly whether the system stops immediately, how incomplete output is handled and how clearly the interruption is communicated. The legal requirement cited is limited to high-risk systems under the EU AI Act; this publication proposes interruption as a broader principle for all conversational AI.

[g] Handover

Design gap: The person is shown as a figure, with no name and no words. Initials are the avatar convention rather than the human one, and named AI personas are common enough now that a name proves nothing — it reads as what the assistant is called. That leaves the whole claim resting on one silhouette, which is thin for a signal this page argues is owed to people. It is recorded as unfinished, not as solved.

[h] Handover

Evidence status: The principle of making AI identity clear is supported by human–AI guidance and by disclosure laws in specific jurisdictions and contexts. Neither the EU AI Act nor California’s B.O.T. Act establishes this visual treatment or a universal duty covering every AI-to-human transfer. The turn-and-settle treatment, and whether it communicates the change of identity clearly enough, require user testing.

[i] Notes

Evidence status: Making relevant system status visible and enabling people to access and correct personal data are established principles. Research does not support a universal claim that progress indicators increase completion; their effects vary with presentation, expectations and context [14][15]. Applying these principles to an adaptive conversational checklist, including its out-of-order filling and additional information, is a design hypothesis requiring user testing. The display is not necessarily a complete account of everything the system stores or processes and must not be presented as one unless it is. Testing should establish whether people understand that scope and whether the display suggests memory beyond the current task or conversation.

[j] Error handling

Evidence status: Clearly signalling an error, explaining it in understandable language and providing a path to recovery are established UX principles. Applying one treatment — stop, fade, add a warning symbol, then blink — across every state is a design hypothesis requiring user testing, particularly whether a frozen mark is understood as an error rather than a stalled interface. The mark keeps its own colour, so the symbol is the only thing that names the state as an error; it and the fade both remain when animation is switched off, but whether they are enough on their own has not been tested.

[k] Waiting and Listening

Accessibility shortcoming: Waiting and Listening are told apart by which element moves — a ring stepping around a still core for Waiting, a still ring around a live core for Listening. When motion is reduced or removed both resolve to an open ring around a small core, and the two states become very hard to distinguish. The difference that remains is ring weight and the width of the opening, and neither has been tested as a sufficient still frame. A difference that does not depend on motion — shape, symbol or accompanying words — is needed before this pair can be relied on in a product.

[l] Working — the Calculating mode

Accessibility shortcoming: Calculating is a counter whose values are meant to read as work rather than as a result, which is why they include arithmetic symbols and never settle on a total. When motion is reduced or removed the wheel stops on a single legible digit — precisely the reading the state is designed to avoid. The still frame contradicts the state’s own rule and needs a different resting treatment.
